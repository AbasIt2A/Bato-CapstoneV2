# Kidney Failure Treatment and Access in the Philippines

## How Filipinos with kidney failure are treated

Among Filipino patients with end-stage renal disease, 94% are on centre-based
haemodialysis, 4% are on peritoneal dialysis, and only 2% have received a
kidney transplant (Pajimna et al., 2023).

## Why so few Filipinos get a transplant

Kidney transplantation is the gold-standard treatment for end-stage renal
disease, but most Filipino patients still choose haemodialysis instead. The
reasons given are the cost of a transplant, low rates of organ donation, a
lack of capable infrastructure, and the need for long-term
immunosuppression therapy afterwards (Pajimna et al., 2023).

## PhilHealth and the Universal Health Care Law

The Philippines relies on PhilHealth, the national health insurance
programme, which under the Universal Health Care Law of 2019 should enrol
every citizen automatically. In practice, registered-beneficiary data from
2018 to 2021 showed geographical gaps, with a few remote provinces at 52%
coverage or below (Pajimna et al., 2023).

## How many dialysis sessions PhilHealth covers

PhilHealth expanded its coverage to 156 haemodialysis sessions per year in
June 2023 — enough to fully cover the recommended three sessions a week. That
was an increase from limits of 45, 90 and 144 sessions set in 2013, 2015 and
2020 respectively, under which patients had to fall back on twice-weekly
sessions or pay out of pocket (Pajimna et al., 2023).

Coverage rules change over time. Confirm the current benefit with PhilHealth
or a local health centre rather than relying on any published figure.

## The PD-First policy

In 2014 PhilHealth introduced a PD-First Z-Benefit Package promoting
peritoneal dialysis as the first-line treatment for eligible patients with
end-stage renal disease, covering up to 90–120 PD solutions a month. In
current practice only three bags a day are actually covered, so patients
prescribed more are either under-dialysed or must pay the difference
themselves (Pajimna et al., 2023).

## Why peritoneal dialysis stays underused

Peritoneal dialysis remains underused in the Philippines despite that policy,
and no local study has yet examined why Filipinos prefer haemodialysis.
Studies elsewhere in Southeast Asia point to fear of peritonitis, the daily
rather than thrice-weekly schedule, and a lack of education about the
treatment. A physician's recommendation strongly influences the choice, and
physicians' own preference for haemodialysis is shaped by a lack of formal
training, of specialised centres, and of incentives (Pajimna et al., 2023).

## Why home-based dialysis is hard to expand here

People prefer centre-based haemodialysis because it is supervised, and because
of patient burnout, family burden, and a lack of confidence in treating
themselves. Home-based treatment would suit the Philippines well, since
haemodialysis centres are concentrated in urbanised cities and geographical
access to healthcare is a problem in rural areas — and home dialysis costs
less in staffing and infrastructure and exposes patients to fewer infections
(Pajimna et al., 2023).

The obstacles are real ones: home haemodialysis is not covered by health
insurance in the Philippines and has no reimbursement policy yet, and patients
in poorer housing conditions may not be eligible to use it at all (Pajimna et
al., 2023).

## Why early detection matters so much here

Every treatment described above is expensive, unevenly available, and
concentrated in cities. That is the practical argument for finding chronic
kidney disease early, while it can still be slowed — long before any of these
choices has to be made.
