# CKD Diagnosis and Management — A Clinical Review

## How common CKD is worldwide

Chronic kidney disease affects between 8% and 16% of the population worldwide
and is often not recognised by either patients or clinicians. It is more
prevalent in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income ones (Chen
et al., 2019).

## How few people know they have it

Fewer than 5% of patients with early CKD report being aware of their disease
(Chen et al., 2019). This is the single fact that most justifies screening: at
the stage when something can still be done, almost nobody knows.

## What causes CKD around the world

Globally, CKD is most commonly attributed to diabetes and high blood pressure.
But other causes are common in Asia, sub-Saharan Africa and many developing
countries — glomerulonephritis, infection, and environmental exposures such as
air pollution, herbal remedies and pesticides (Chen et al., 2019).

This matters for Filipino readers: the two big causes are the same everywhere,
but the additional causes seen across Asia mean diabetes and high blood
pressure are not the whole picture here.

## How CKD is usually found

Chronic kidney disease is typically picked up through routine testing of blood
chemistry and urine, or as an incidental finding when someone is being
investigated for something else — not because the person came in complaining
about their kidneys (Chen et al., 2019).

## Symptoms that can occur

Less commonly, people may notice gross blood in the urine, "foamy urine" (a
sign of albumin in the urine), needing to pass urine at night, flank pain, or
passing less urine than usual. If CKD is advanced, people may report fatigue,
poor appetite, nausea, vomiting, a metallic taste, unintentional weight loss,
itching, changes in mental state, breathlessness, or swelling in the legs and
feet (Chen et al., 2019).

## Who guidelines say should be screened

A risk-based approach to screening is suggested by many clinical practice
guidelines, with screening recommended for people older than 60 years or with
a history of diabetes or high blood pressure. Screening is also worth
considering in people with autoimmune disease, obesity, kidney stones,
recurrent urinary tract infections, reduced kidney mass, exposure to certain
medicines, and previous episodes of acute kidney injury (Chen et al., 2019).

The review is candid about a limitation: no randomised clinical trial has yet
shown that screening people without symptoms for CKD improves their outcomes.

## Does CKD always lead to dialysis

No. Most people with CKD do not require kidney replacement therapy during
their lifetime (Chen et al., 2019).

Risk of progression varies a great deal between individuals, and validated
tools exist that clinicians use to estimate it from age, sex, eGFR and urine
ACR. The review notes that such risk estimates are as useful for *reassuring*
people with mild CKD as for identifying those at high risk.

## When a kidney specialist gets involved

Guidelines recommend referral to a nephrologist when eGFR falls below 30
mL/min/1.73m² or when urine ACR rises above 300 mg per 24 hours. Referral is
also indicated for rapid decline in kidney function, uncontrolled high blood
pressure despite several medicines, persistent potassium abnormalities,
anemia needing treatment, recurrent or extensive kidney stones, hereditary
kidney disease, and acute kidney injury (Chen et al., 2019).

## How the decision to start dialysis is made

The decision to begin kidney replacement therapy is based on a person's
symptoms, not on their GFR number alone. Dialysis is considered when someone
has symptoms of uraemia, electrolyte abnormalities, or fluid overload that
does not respond to other treatment. The review stresses shared
decision-making: people should be educated about the options early and take an
active part in choosing (Chen et al., 2019).

Kidney transplantation is considered the optimal therapy for end-stage kidney
disease, with the best outcomes when a living-donor transplant happens before
or shortly after dialysis begins. The alternatives are in-centre
haemodialysis, home haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or conservative care
without dialysis — and a preference for conservative management should be
discussed and honoured (Chen et al., 2019).

## Why CKD is worth catching early

Progressive CKD is associated with end-stage kidney disease, cardiovascular
disease and increased mortality, which is why early detection and treatment
matters. CKD is the 16th leading cause of years of life lost worldwide (Chen
et al., 2019).
