# The SCORED Screening Instrument (Bang et al., 2007)

## What the SCORED tool is

SCORED (SCreening for Occult REnal Disease) is a validated nine-question
scoring tool (Bang et al., 2007) that estimates the chance a person already
has reduced kidney function without knowing it. It uses only age, sex and
medical history — no blood test and no urine test are needed to take it.

It was developed on 8,530 adults in the US National Health and Nutrition
Examination Survey and externally validated in the Atherosclerosis Risk in
Communities study. Throughout the study, "reduced kidney function" means an
estimated filtering rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73m² (SCORED).

## The nine things the SCORED questions ask about

The questionnaire asks about nine things: older age, female sex, anemia, high
blood pressure, diabetes, a history of heart attack or stroke, heart failure,
peripheral vascular disease, and protein in the urine (SCORED). Age is scored
in bands; the other eight are simple yes/no statements.

## How the SCORED total is worked out

Each statement that is true for a person adds points. A statement that is not
true — or that the person is unsure about — adds zero. The points are added up
for a total between 0 and 12 (SCORED).

- Age adds the most: 2 points at 50–59 years, 3 points at 60–69, and 4 points
  at 70 or older. Being under 50 adds nothing.
- Each of the other eight adds 1 point: being a woman, anemia, high blood
  pressure, diabetes, a history of heart attack or stroke, heart failure,
  circulation disease in the legs, and protein in the urine.

These weights come from the study's final model, in which age carried by far
the largest effect on the chance of having reduced kidney function (SCORED).

## Older age and kidney risk

This screening question asks how old you are, in years. Age is asked
because kidney function naturally declines as people grow older, so age
helps estimate risk even in someone who feels perfectly well.

Risk rises from age 50 and is higher at 60 and at 70 and above, and the
SCORED tool counts more points at each older band (SCORED; Bang et al.,
2007). Being older does not mean a person has kidney disease — it only
raises the chance that getting checked is worthwhile.

## Female sex and kidney risk

In the SCORED study population, women had a slightly higher chance of
reduced kidney function.

## What a Screen Positive result means

A total score of 4 or more is "Screen Positive". It does NOT mean the person
has kidney disease. In the study population, about 1 in 5 people who scored 4
or more actually had reduced kidney function (SCORED).

What it means is that a confirmatory test with a health professional is
recommended — a simple serum creatinine blood test, used to estimate GFR.
Only a health professional using laboratory tests can determine for certain
whether kidney disease is present.

## What a Screen Negative result means

A total score of 0–3 is "Screen Negative": reduced kidney function is unlikely
right now. It is not a guarantee of future kidney health. The tool's authors
recommend re-taking the survey about once a year (SCORED).

## How accurate the SCORED tool is

Accuracy figures are population-level: they describe the tool, not any one
person. At the cutoff score of 4, the tool correctly flags about 92% of people
who truly have reduced kidney function (sensitivity), and about 68% of people
without it are correctly not flagged (specificity). About 1 in 5 Screen
Positive people actually have reduced kidney function (positive predictive
value 18%), and a negative screen is correct about 99% of the time (negative
predictive value) (SCORED).

The cutoff of 4 was chosen deliberately to favour catching cases over avoiding
false alarms, because the follow-up test is cheap, safe and reliable. A
stricter cutoff of 6 would flag fewer people correctly (sensitivity 68%) while
wrongly flagging fewer of the healthy (specificity 87%) (SCORED).

## What the SCORED tool cannot tell you

The tool is weighted toward the common causes of kidney disease — older age,
diabetes and high blood pressure, along with heart disease and anemia. Two
consequences follow, and the study states both (SCORED).

First, a high proportion of older people will screen positive, especially
above 70. Second, the tool is not designed to pick up less common kidney
conditions such as polycystic kidney disease or glomerulonephritis, and it
asks nothing about blood in the urine or about family history of kidney
failure. A Screen Negative result therefore does not rule those out.
