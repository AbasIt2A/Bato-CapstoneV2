# Medicines Used in Kidney Care

## Stopping or changing a prescribed medicine

Do not stop a prescribed medicine, or change its dose, without talking with
your health care team first (NKF). Many kidney-related medicines are meant
to be taken long term, as prescribed (NIDDK).

Feeling better does not always mean a medicine is no longer needed. High
blood pressure, for example, usually has no signs or symptoms (CDC), so
feeling well is not a reliable sign that it is under control.

Your doctor may change your medicines over time — for example taking a
medicine less often, taking a smaller dose, or switching to a different
one — because kidneys that filter less well can let some medicines build up
in the body (NIDDK). Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether any of your
medicines or doses need to change (NIDDK).

## How medicine doses are decided

There is no single safe amount of a medicine that fits everyone with kidney
disease. How many milligrams of a medicine such as lisinopril, losartan, or
metformin is safe depends on the person, so the right medicine and dose are
decided by a doctor for each person. With
kidney disease, many medicines are removed from the body by the kidneys, so
a doctor may use a lower dose or space doses further apart, and checks
kidney function (eGFR) with blood tests to decide when to adjust (NKF).

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every medicine you take, including
medicines bought without a prescription, vitamins, and supplements. Using
one pharmacy and keeping an up-to-date list of your medicines helps them
check for problems (NIDDK).

## ACE inhibitors and ARBs (such as losartan)

ACE inhibitors and ARBs are prescription blood pressure medicines that also
protect the kidneys. They can lower the amount of protein (albumin) in the
urine, slow kidney damage, and lower the risk of heart attack and stroke
(NKF). They may slow kidney disease and delay kidney failure even in people
who do not have high blood pressure (NIDDK).

ACE inhibitor names usually end in "-pril", such as lisinopril, ramipril,
benazepril, and quinapril. ARB names usually end in "-sartan", such as
losartan, valsartan, irbesartan, and olmesartan. An ACE inhibitor and an ARB
should not be taken together (NKF).

Possible side effects include dizziness or weakness from low blood pressure,
a dry cough (more common with ACE inhibitors), and high potassium, which is
why blood tests are done. A small early drop in eGFR can happen and does not
necessarily mean kidney disease is getting worse. Rarely, they cause
swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, which needs emergency care
right away. They are not used in pregnancy (NKF).

The dose is chosen and adjusted by your doctor. Do not change your dose or
stop taking it unless you talk with your health care team first (NKF).

## SGLT2 inhibitors (such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin)

SGLT2 inhibitors were first used for diabetes, but studies found that they
also protect the kidneys and heart, even in people without diabetes. They
lower the pressure inside the kidney's filters, which may slow chronic
kidney disease and lower the risk of kidney failure and heart failure
flare-ups (NKF).

Examples include dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin, and
ertugliflozin (NKF).

Possible side effects include peeing more, which can lead to fluid loss and
low blood pressure; genital and urinary infections; and low blood sugar,
mainly in people who also take insulin or certain other diabetes medicines.
Because of a rare risk called ketoacidosis, the care team may pause them
before surgery or a long fast. When one is started, doses of other diabetes,
blood pressure, or water pill medicines sometimes need adjusting (NKF).

## Finerenone and medicines that can raise potassium

Finerenone is another medicine that protects the kidneys. Like ACE
inhibitors and ARBs, it can raise the potassium level in the blood. The
related medicines spironolactone and eplerenone can also raise potassium
(NKF). This is one reason people taking these medicines have regular blood
tests.

## Diuretics (water pills)

Diuretics, sometimes called water pills, help the body get rid of extra
fluid and are often used to help control blood pressure in kidney disease
(NIDDK). Furosemide and torsemide are examples. If the dose is too high for
the person, diuretics can cause dehydration or low potassium (NKF).

## Diabetes medicines and kidney function

Some diabetes medicines, including metformin, glyburide, glimepiride, and
insulin, can build up in the body as kidney function falls. A doctor may
lower the dose, avoid a medicine when kidney function is low, or switch to a
different one; a dose that is too high for the kidneys can raise the risk of
low blood sugar (NKF). Do not change diabetes medicines on your own.

## Statins (cholesterol medicines)

Chronic kidney disease raises the risk of heart attack and stroke. Statins
lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol and help protect the heart, and guidelines
recommend them for many people with CKD — for example most adults over 50
who are not on dialysis (NKF).

Examples include atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, and pravastatin.
Possible side effects include muscle aches, stomach upset, and, rarely,
liver problems, which are checked with blood tests. Some antibiotics and
antifungal medicines can interact with statins. Do not change your statin
dose or stop taking it unless you talk with your health care team first
(NKF).

## Medicines for anemia in kidney disease

Anemia linked to kidney disease is treated by first looking for causes such
as low iron or low vitamin B12 or folate. Treatment can include iron, as a
pill or an infusion into a vein, and vitamin B12 or folate if the body lacks
them (NIDDK).

A doctor may also prescribe an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA), a
medicine that helps the body make red blood cells. It is given as an
injection. ESAs may ease symptoms and help avoid blood transfusions, but
they are not right for everyone with kidney disease and anemia. Talk with
your doctor about the risks and benefits (NIDDK).

## Herbal supplements and "kidney detox" products

Herbal supplements can harm the kidneys, interact with prescription
medicines, or worsen problems such as high potassium. They are not tested
for dose, content, or purity the way medicines are, and some have been
found to contain metals such as lead or mercury or ingredients that damage
the kidneys (NKF).

Products sold as a "kidney detox" or "kidney cleanse", and "proprietary
blends" that do not list their exact contents, are best avoided. Herbs
named as risky for people with kidney disease include licorice root,
St. John's wort, comfrey, Aristolochia, astragalus, and many others; some
products such as noni, coconut water, turmeric, and seaweed or kelp are high
in potassium or phosphorus. Talk with your care team before starting any
supplement, and bring the bottle to your appointments (NKF).

## Heartburn medicines, antacids, and bowel preparations

Some heartburn and stomach medicines can build up in people with kidney
disease. Ask a health professional before taking acid reducers such as
famotidine or cimetidine, or an over-the-counter proton pump inhibitor
(PPI). Antacids containing calcium, magnesium, bismuth, or aluminum can
also accumulate, and people with kidney disease should avoid products that
contain aluminum (NKF).

Oral sodium phosphate bowel preparations, used before some procedures, can
cause kidney failure and should be avoided by people with kidney disease;
other kinds of bowel preparation can be used instead (NKF).

## Medicines on sick days

Being dehydrated — for example from vomiting or diarrhea — while taking
blood pressure medicines, especially together with NSAID pain relievers,
can lead to sudden kidney injury (NIDDK). Before you get sick, ask your
doctor whether you should stop any medicines during an illness, and when to
restart them (NIDDK).
