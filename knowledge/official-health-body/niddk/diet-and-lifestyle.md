# Diet and Lifestyle with CKD

## Food, drink, and CKD

- Less salt helps control blood pressure — eat fewer processed, canned,
  salty, and instant foods (WHO, NIDDK).
- Protein: the body needs it, but eating very large amounts makes the
  kidneys work harder. People with CKD may be advised on how much protein
  is right for them by their doctor or a dietitian (NIDDK).
- In later stages, a doctor or dietitian may advise limits on potassium,
  phosphorus, or fluids. These limits are individual — no one should
  restrict these on their own (NIDDK).
- For healthy kidneys, drinking enough water is good practice; a person on
  dialysis or with advanced CKD may instead have a fluid limit set by their
  care team (NIDDK).

## Living with CKD

- Many people with CKD — especially in early stages — live long, full
  lives. The keys are regular check-ups, taking prescribed medicines, and
  the healthy habits below (NIDDK, CDC).
- Know your numbers: eGFR, urine albumin, and blood pressure, and how they
  change over time (KDIGO).
- Tell every doctor and pharmacist you have CKD before taking any new
  medicine, and be cautious with herbal or "detox" supplements (NIDDK).

## Body weight and a healthy weight for your kidneys

- There is no single "kidney weight" and no one number that is healthy for
  everyone. A healthy weight depends on your height and build, and health
  workers usually judge it with body mass index (BMI), which compares weight
  with height. Only a doctor or a dietitian, who can weigh you and see your
  test results, can say what a healthy weight is for you (NIDDK, KDOQI).
- Carrying too much weight matters for the kidneys. Obesity is listed
  alongside diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, family history and
  older age among the things that raise the risk of chronic kidney disease
  (CDC).
- Much of that risk is indirect: extra weight makes type 2 diabetes and high
  blood pressure more likely, and those two conditions are the most common
  causes of kidney failure (CDC).
- In people who already have kidney disease, obesity is associated with
  faster progression toward kidney failure, which is why weight is part of
  protecting the kidney function a person still has (APSN, 2025).
- What to do about it is ordinary advice, not a special kidney regimen: make
  healthy food choices, be more active, aim for a healthy weight, and manage
  the conditions that damage kidneys. CDC puts the same thing as "lose weight
  if it is needed", next to controlling blood pressure and blood sugar
  (NIDDK, CDC).
- Do not try to lose weight through crash dieting, "detox" products, or very
  high-protein plans. Anyone with CKD should ask a doctor or dietitian before
  changing how they eat, because protein and fluid needs shift with the stage
  of kidney disease (NIDDK, KDOQI).
- In advanced CKD and on dialysis, weight is watched for a second reason:
  losing weight without meaning to can be a sign of poor nutrition, and extra
  fluid in the body changes what the scale says. Weight goals there are set
  by the care team, not decided alone (KDOQI, 2020).

## Keeping kidneys healthy

- Control blood pressure and blood sugar; take prescribed maintenance
  medicines consistently (WHO, CDC, NIDDK).
- Drink enough water; eat less salt and processed food; keep a healthy
  weight; stay physically active; do not smoke.
- Avoid regularly taking pain relievers like NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen,
  mefenamic acid) without medical advice — overuse can harm the kidneys.
- Have regular check-ups if you have diabetes, hypertension, heart disease,
  or a family history of kidney disease.
