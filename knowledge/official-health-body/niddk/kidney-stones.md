# Kidney Stones: Signs, Diagnosis, and Medicines

## How kidney stones are identified

A kidney stone is a hard piece of material that can form when certain minerals
build up in urine. Possible signs include sharp pain in the back, side, lower
abdomen or groin; blood in the urine; painful or frequent urination; and nausea
or vomiting. Some small stones cause no symptoms. These signs can also have
other causes, so symptoms alone cannot confirm a kidney stone.

A health professional uses a medical history, physical exam, and tests to
diagnose kidney stones. Urine and blood tests may help; imaging such as an
abdominal X-ray or CT scan can show a stone and whether it blocks urine flow.
Someone with fever, chills, inability to urinate, or severe pain should seek
medical care promptly.

## Medicines for kidney stones and their risks

A health professional may prescribe pain medicine for a stone or medicine to
prevent future stones. Prevention medicines vary by stone type and can include
potassium citrate, diuretics, allopurinol, or antibiotics for certain stones.
Some kidney-stone medicines can have minor or serious side effects. The risks
depend on the particular medicine and the person's health history; side effects
are more likely with higher doses or longer use. Tell a health professional
about side effects. Without the medicine's name, its specific risks cannot be
identified.

## What types of kidney stones are there?

Kidney stones can be made of different materials. Calcium stones, including
calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate, are the most common. Other types include
uric acid, struvite, and cystine stones. Struvite stones may form after a
urinary tract infection; cystine stones are linked to an inherited condition.
Finding out the stone type can help a health professional plan prevention.

Source: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/definition-facts

## How are kidney stones treated?

Treatment depends on a stone's size, location, and type. Small stones may pass
without a procedure. Larger stones, stones blocking urine, or stones causing
great pain may need urgent treatment. A urologist may break a stone with shock
waves, remove or break it with a scope passed through the urinary tract, or
remove it through a small cut in the back. A health professional decides which
approach fits the person; someone who is vomiting or dehydrated may need
hospital care.

Source: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/treatment

## Can diet help prevent another kidney stone?

Drinking enough fluid, mainly water, can help prevent kidney stones, but the
right amount depends on the person's health and may be restricted with kidney
failure. Food changes depend on the stone type. A health professional may
suggest changes to sodium, animal protein, calcium, or oxalate, and a dietitian
can help plan meals. People who have had a stone can ask what type it was
before changing their diet; cutting out all calcium is not a general rule for
calcium stones.
