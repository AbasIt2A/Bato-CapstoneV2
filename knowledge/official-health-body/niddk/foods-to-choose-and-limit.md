# Which Foods to Choose and Limit with CKD

## There is no single CKD diet

There is no single meal plan that is right for everyone with CKD (NIDDK).

In the early stages, a person may have few limits on what they eat and drink.
As CKD advances and nutritional needs change, more changes to the diet may be
needed. A health care professional may suggest working with a registered
dietitian to build an eating plan for an individual's own needs (NIDDK).

What follows is general information about food groups, not a plan for any one
person. How much of anything a specific person should eat is set by their
doctor or dietitian.

## Salty and processed foods to limit

Most of the salt people eat does not come from the saltshaker — it comes from
packaged, prepared and restaurant food. Even food that does not taste salty can
be high in sodium (NIDDK).

Foods NIDDK names as high in salt or sodium:

- canned foods
- some frozen foods
- most processed meats
- snack foods such as chips and crackers
- table salt, some seasonings, ketchup and mustard
- sauces such as barbecue, soy and teriyaki

Ways to use less salt (NIDDK):

- limit prepared, packaged and fast food
- choose unprocessed meats instead of processed meats
- eat more fresh, frozen and canned fruits and vegetables that have no added
  sauce, seasoning, salt or sodium
- drain and rinse canned vegetables, beans, meat and fish with water to remove
  extra salt
- cook from scratch, so you control what goes in
- use spices, herbs and sodium-free seasonings instead of salt — lemon juice,
  herbs and hot peppers are alternatives

Taste adjusts. Reducing salt slowly over time makes the change less noticeable
(NIDDK).

## Foods with added phosphorus to watch for

Phosphorus occurs naturally in protein-rich foods such as meat, dairy products
and beans. But phosphorus is also ADDED to many foods, and those additives are
the main source of phosphorus for many people (NIDDK).

To take in less phosphorus (NIDDK):

- limit or avoid packaged and processed foods and drinks with added phosphorus,
  such as deli meats and flavoured drinks
- read the ingredient list for "phosphorus" or words containing "PHOS" —
  phosphoric acid, disodium phosphate, monosodium phosphate
- eat larger portions of fruits and vegetables, which are naturally low in
  phosphorus, and smaller portions of foods high in it
- ask at the meat counter which meats have no added phosphorus — some packaged
  meat and poultry does

The body absorbs more phosphorus from animal-based foods such as meat and
poultry than from plant-based foods such as beans and seeds (NIDDK).

## Keeping potassium in the right range

Whether a person needs to limit potassium at all depends on their blood
potassium level, which a health care professional checks with a blood test.
Both too little and too much potassium cause heart and muscle problems, so this
is not something to adjust alone (NIDDK).

Where a person's potassium is high, NIDDK's food advice is (NIDDK):

- watch serving sizes — a large serving of a lower-potassium food can carry
  more potassium than a small serving of a higher-potassium one
- use spices and herbs instead of SALT SUBSTITUTES, which can be high in
  potassium
- limit foods with potassium chloride on the ingredient list; it is sometimes
  used in place of salt in packaged foods
- drain canned fruit and vegetables and throw away the liquid, which is often
  high in potassium
- boiling and leaching are cooking methods that lower the potassium in certain
  vegetables
- for a person with diabetes treating a low blood sugar, apple, grape or
  cranberry juice is lower in potassium than orange juice

## Heart-healthy fats and how to cook

People with CKD have a higher risk of heart and blood vessel disease, so
replacing saturated and trans fats with unsaturated fats protects the heart
(NIDDK).

- Unsaturated fats are found in canola oil, safflower oil, avocados, nuts and
  seeds (NIDDK).
- Saturated fats are found in animal products such as red meat, poultry and
  butter, and are usually solid at room temperature (NIDDK).
- Trans fats are found in fast food, fried food and commercially produced baked
  goods (NIDDK).

Cooking methods that use less fat: grill, broil, bake, roast, air-fry or
stir-fry instead of deep-frying. Cook with nonstick spray or a small amount of
olive oil instead of butter, and trim fat from meat and remove skin from
poultry before cooking (NIDDK).

NIDDK also advises limiting alcohol, and asking a health care professional how
much alcohol is safe for you (NIDDK).

## Protein, and why the amount is not something to set yourself

Protein builds and maintains muscle, bone, skin, organs and blood, and helps
fight disease and heal wounds. As the body uses protein it breaks down into
waste that the kidneys must remove (NIDDK).

Some people with CKD may need to eat moderate amounts of protein so that waste
does not build up — but too little protein leads to malnutrition. Both
directions carry harm, which is why the amount is worked out with a dietitian
or health care professional rather than chosen alone (NIDDK).

Where a reduction is advised, NIDDK's general shape of it is to limit meat and
dairy and to get more protein from plant-based foods, choosing lean or low-fat
meats that are also low in phosphorus. Someone who eats no animal products
should eat a variety of plant proteins (NIDDK).

People on dialysis may need MORE protein, not less, because dialysis removes
protein from the blood — peritoneal dialysis removes more than hemodialysis
(NIDDK).

## Eating enough, and why losing weight can be a problem

Food gives the body energy, measured in calories. Some people with CKD stop
eating because they have no appetite or because food does not taste the same
(NIDDK).

This matters in the opposite direction to what people often assume: consuming
fewer calories than the body uses causes weight loss, and losing too much
weight can make a person sicker. Anyone who finds they do not feel like eating
should talk to a dietitian or health care professional about getting enough
calories from nutritious sources (NIDDK).

## Liquids

The body needs liquids to work properly, but people with CKD may need to limit
how much they drink, because damaged kidneys cannot remove extra fluid (NIDDK).

How much liquid is right is set individually with a health care professional
and depends on kidney function. Contact a health care professional about any
swelling in the face, arms, legs or abdomen (NIDDK).
