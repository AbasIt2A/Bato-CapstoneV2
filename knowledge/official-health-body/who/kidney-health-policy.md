# Global Kidney Health Policy and Burden

## How many people have kidney disease worldwide

About 674 million people live with chronic kidney disease — around 9% of the
world's population (WHO, resolution WHA78.6, 2025).

## Kidney disease as a growing cause of death

Kidney disease is one of the fastest-growing causes of death globally and is
projected to become the fifth leading cause of death by 2050. Without action,
the age-standardised death rate is projected to rise by 33% (WHO, WHA78.6,
2025).

## How often kidney disease accompanies diabetes and high blood pressure

Kidney disease is present in 1 in 5 people with high blood pressure and occurs
in 4 out of 10 people with diabetes. It contributes to 1 in 10 deaths
attributed to high blood pressure (WHO, WHA78.6, 2025).

This is why the two conditions matter so much in kidney screening: they are
both the leading causes of CKD and the commonest settings in which it is
found.

## Why access to kidney care is unequal

Access to kidney care is highly unequal worldwide, especially in
resource-limited settings, where kidney disease often goes undiagnosed and
untreated. In many developing countries people must pay out of pocket for
kidney care, because only a small percentage of these countries cover dialysis
and transplantation — which makes kidney disease a leading cause of
catastrophic health spending for a household (WHO, WHA78.6, 2025).

## What the World Health Assembly asked countries to do

The Seventy-eighth World Health Assembly urged member states to build
prevention, early detection and management of kidney disease into national
health policies and universal health coverage packages; to strengthen control
of diabetes and high blood pressure so that CKD is prevented or delayed; to
monitor kidney disease burden in national health information systems; and to
raise public awareness and health literacy about kidney disease (WHO, WHA78.6,
2025).

## Kidney disease and infections

Kidney disease is a preventable but potentially fatal consequence of many
infections, including malaria, dengue fever, HIV, tuberculosis, hepatitis,
sepsis, COVID-19, diarrhoeal illnesses and neglected tropical diseases (WHO,
WHA78.6, 2025).

## Kidney disease and pregnancy

Kidney disease is both a significant risk factor for and a consequence of high
blood pressure disorders of pregnancy, which affect about 1 in 10 pregnancies.
Both are associated with maternal deaths and with preterm and low-birth-weight
babies, who are themselves at higher risk of kidney disease later (WHO,
WHA78.6, 2025).

## World Kidney Day

World Kidney Day is held every year on the second Thursday of March to raise
public awareness of kidney health (WHO, WHA78.6, 2025).

## The WHO PEN package for primary health care

The WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions (PEN) sets
out a minimum set of cost-effective interventions that primary health care can
deliver even in low-resource settings, covering the detection, diagnosis,
treatment and care of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and chronic respiratory
disease, along with healthy-lifestyle counselling and self-care. Its
components can be delivered by primary care doctors and by non-physician
health workers, and countries adapt it to their own needs and resources (WHO,
PEN, 2020).

PEN matters for kidney health because the two conditions it targets most
directly — diabetes and high blood pressure — are the two leading causes of
chronic kidney disease.
