# CKD Basics and Awareness

## What healthy kidneys do

Each kidney is about the size of a computer mouse, and together they filter
all the blood in the body about every 30 minutes, removing wastes, toxins and
extra fluid. They also help control blood pressure, signal the body to make
red blood cells, help keep bones healthy, and regulate blood chemicals that
are essential to life (CDC).

## How many people have CKD without knowing it

More than 1 in 7 American adults has chronic kidney disease, and as many as
9 in 10 of them do not know they have it. About 40% of people with severely
reduced kidney function who are not on dialysis are unaware of it (CDC).

These are United States figures. They are quoted here because they show how
commonly CKD goes unnoticed, not as an estimate for any other country.

## Why CKD is silent

People with CKD may not feel ill or notice any symptoms at all. The only way
to find out for sure is through blood and urine tests, which measure the
creatinine level in the blood and protein in the urine (CDC).

## Why CKD matters to the rest of the body

Because damaged kidneys cannot filter properly, excess fluid and waste stay
in the body and can cause other health problems. CKD is linked to anemia, more
frequent infections, low calcium and high potassium and phosphorus levels in
the blood, loss of appetite, and depression or reduced quality of life. It
also increases the risk of heart disease and stroke (CDC).

## Does CKD always get worse

CKD has varying levels of seriousness and usually gets worse over time, but
treatment has been shown to slow it down. Not everyone with kidney disease
progresses to kidney failure (CDC).

## What raises the risk of CKD

Diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, obesity, a family history of
CKD, and older age all raise the risk of chronic kidney disease. In the United
States, diabetes and high blood pressure are the leading causes of kidney
failure, accounting for 2 out of 3 new cases (CDC).

## Steps that protect the kidneys

- Keep blood pressure below 140/90 mm Hg, or whatever target a doctor sets.
- If you have diabetes, stay in your target blood sugar range as much as
  possible.
- Be physically active — it helps control both blood pressure and blood sugar.
- Lose weight if it is needed.
- Get tested for CKD regularly if you are at risk.
- If you have CKD, ask to see a dietitian about a kidney-healthy eating plan.
- Take medicines as instructed by your own doctor.
- If you smoke, make a plan to quit — smoking can worsen kidney disease and
  interfere with blood-pressure medicine.

(CDC)

## How common kidney failure treatment is

About 360 people in the United States begin treatment for kidney failure —
dialysis or a kidney transplant — every day, and an estimated 35.5 million US
adults have CKD, most of them undiagnosed (CDC).
