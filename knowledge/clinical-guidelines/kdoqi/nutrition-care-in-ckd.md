# Nutrition Care in CKD

## Why kidney diet advice is individual, not general

There is no single CKD diet. What a person with kidney disease should eat
depends on their stage of CKD, on their blood test results, on whether they
are on dialysis, and on whether they have diabetes — and specialist nutrition
guidance sets different targets for each of those situations (KDOQI, 2020).

This is why BATO gives no gram limits, no fluid volumes and no
potassium or phosphorus targets. Those belong to a doctor or a kidney
dietitian who can see your test results.

## Why dietary limits are prescribed under supervision

The dietary changes recommended in kidney disease — including reducing protein
— are specified in the guideline as things to be prescribed and monitored
under close clinical supervision. Reducing protein intake can harm the
nutritional status of someone already at risk of protein-energy wasting, so it
is not a change to make on your own initiative (KDOQI, 2020).

## Why the advice changes as CKD changes

The right nutrition advice for one person changes over the course of their
illness, and can even reverse. The guideline gives the example of potassium: a
person at stage 2 CKD may have *low* potassium and need supplements and a
high-potassium diet, while years later at stage 4 the same person may have
*high* potassium and need to restrict it instead. If they then receive a
kidney transplant, they may need neither (KDOQI, 2020).

Advice you were given before — or that helped someone else with kidney disease
— may therefore be wrong for you now. This is what makes ongoing monitoring by
a kidney dietitian part of the care, rather than a one-off consultation.

## The role of the kidney dietitian

Nutrition care in CKD is treated as medical nutrition therapy, needed at every
stage of CKD and delivered with ongoing monitoring by a registered dietitian
working in kidney care, because the metabolic, acid-base, fluid and
electrolyte balances that the diet has to match keep shifting as CKD
progresses (KDOQI, 2020).

## Reduced salt as a general direction

Reducing dietary sodium is suggested as an additional lifestyle change for
adults with CKD, to help control fluid balance and body weight (KDOQI, 2020).
How much to reduce it by is an individual matter for a clinician to set.
