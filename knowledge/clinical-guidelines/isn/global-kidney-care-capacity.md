# Global Capacity to Deliver Kidney Care

## The gap between needing dialysis and getting it

The gap between the number of people who need kidney replacement therapy —
dialysis or a transplant — and the number who actually receive it is far wider
in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income ones. The main reasons
are a lack of public funding and out-of-pocket costs that patients cannot meet
(ISN, Global Kidney Health Atlas, 2023).

## How many countries pay for dialysis and transplants

About two-thirds of countries worldwide (63%) provide public funding for
haemodialysis. Fewer fund the alternatives: 55% publicly fund peritoneal
dialysis and 59% fund kidney transplantation. In 37% of countries surveyed,
the cost of kidney replacement therapy is privately funded and paid entirely
out of pocket (ISN, 2023).

## The shortage of kidney doctors

The median number of nephrologists worldwide is about 11.75 per million
people, and the density in high-income countries is more than 80 times higher
than in low-income countries. Of the world's nephrologists, 5.8% treat
children and 35% are women (ISN, 2023).

## What people living with kidney disease say gets in their way

Asked directly, people living with kidney disease named a lack of effective
government policies (70%) and the excessive cost of kidney replacement therapy
(45%) as the obstacles to receiving good care (ISN, 2023).

## What has improved

Compared with the previous edition of the Atlas, the number of treatment
centres has grown — haemodialysis centres by 9.8%, peritoneal dialysis by 13%
and kidney transplantation by 7% — and the density of nephrologists rose from
9.5 to 12.4 per million people between 2019 and 2023. Public funding also
expanded, particularly for peritoneal dialysis (ISN, 2023).

## What the Atlas recommends

The Atlas calls for public funding of kidney replacement therapy in low-income
and lower-middle-income countries, for addressing workforce shortages through
multidisciplinary teams, task-shifting so that primary care practitioners take
a larger role, and telemedicine — and above all for preventing kidney failure
in the first place through detection programmes for acute kidney injury and
CKD, since supporting people with CKD before dialysis is what slows the
disease down (ISN, 2023).
