# Diabetes and Kidney Disease in the Asia-Pacific

## What diabetic kidney disease is

Diabetic kidney disease is kidney damage caused by diabetes. People with it
are at high risk of kidney failure, of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease,
of heart failure, and of dying earlier than they otherwise would (APSN, 2025).

## How often diabetes affects the kidneys in this region

Among people living with type 2 diabetes in the Asia-Pacific, an estimated
17.0%–18.2% have moderately increased albuminuria. Estimates for severely
increased albuminuria range from 2.1% to 14.4%, and for reduced eGFR from
15.3% to 61.6% — a very wide range, reflecting how differently the studies
were done (APSN, 2025).

The guideline notes plainly that while the prevalence of diabetes itself is
well reported, the prevalence of diabetic kidney disease in the Asia-Pacific
is **not** well documented, and there is little or no data for people with
type 1 diabetes in these countries.

## When someone with diabetes should be screened for kidney disease

APSN suggests annual screening for diabetic kidney disease, beginning 5 years
after a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes and immediately at the diagnosis of type
2 diabetes (APSN, 2025).

Type 2 diabetes is screened from the moment it is diagnosed because it often
goes undetected for years before that, so kidney damage may already be present
on the day of diagnosis.

## What test is used, and what to do when it is not available

The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio is the recommended test for detecting
diabetic kidney disease early. But its availability and cost limit its use in
lower- and middle-income countries, so the guideline's judgement is that some
form of albuminuria detection is far better than none: where UACR is not
available, a spot urine albumin concentration, a urine protein-to-creatinine
ratio, or even a urine dipstick can be used as an alternative screening method
(APSN, 2025).

If a test you were offered is not the one named as ideal, that does not make
it worthless — this guideline was written with exactly that situation in mind.

## Why lifestyle care comes first

Lifestyle changes are cost-effective at both preventing and managing diabetic
kidney disease. Educating people with diabetes about stopping smoking, weight
reduction, exercise and dietary change reduces the risks of the large- and
small-vessel complications of diabetes and slows the decline of kidney
function in those who already have kidney damage. These can be delivered at
low cost in any setting, and the guideline holds that they should form the
foundation of care everywhere — a foundation it warns is easily overlooked
amid the excitement about newer, more expensive therapies (APSN, 2025).

## Electronic cigarettes and kidney disease

Using electronic cigarettes as a way of giving up conventional tobacco should
be approached with caution in people with chronic kidney disease, and avoided
altogether in those who also have lung or cardiovascular disease (APSN, 2025).

## Not every kidney problem in a person with diabetes is caused by diabetes

People with diabetes can have kidney disease from a different cause
altogether. Across studies of kidney biopsies in people with diabetes, the
proportion whose kidney disease was actually diabetic kidney disease varied
enormously, from 7% to 94% of cases, with non-diabetic kidney disease
accounting for 3% to 83% (APSN, 2025). Establishing what is actually causing
the damage is a job for a specialist.
