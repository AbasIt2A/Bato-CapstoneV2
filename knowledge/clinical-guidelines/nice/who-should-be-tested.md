# Who Should Be Tested for CKD

## Which adults should be offered a kidney test

NICE advises offering a CKD test — both an estimated GFR from a blood test and
an ACR from a urine test — to any adult with any of these (NICE, NG203):

- diabetes
- high blood pressure
- a previous episode of acute kidney injury
- cardiovascular disease, meaning ischaemic heart disease, chronic heart
  failure, peripheral vascular disease or cerebrovascular disease
- structural disease of the urinary tract, recurrent kidney stones, or an
  enlarged prostate
- multisystem diseases that can involve the kidneys, such as systemic lupus
  erythematosus
- gout
- a family history of end-stage kidney disease or of hereditary kidney disease
- blood or protein found in the urine incidentally

This is guidance written for the UK's National Health Service. In the
Philippines, the decision about who to test and how often rests with a
person's own doctor or health centre.

## Both tests, not just one

NICE asks for eGFR from creatinine **and** ACR together whenever CKD testing is
offered. Testing only the blood or only the urine can miss kidney damage that
the other test would have found (NICE, NG203).

## Why one abnormal result is not a diagnosis

An eGFR result below 60 mL/min/1.73m² in an adult who has not been tested
before should be confirmed by repeating the test within 2 weeks. Serum
creatinine varies both biologically and analytically — by roughly ±5% — and
that variation has to be allowed for when interpreting a change in eGFR (NICE,
NG203).

## When eGFR should be read with caution

Estimated GFR should be interpreted with caution in people with unusually high
or low muscle mass — for example bodybuilders, people who have had an
amputation, and people with muscle-wasting conditions. Reduced muscle mass
leads to overestimating GFR, and increased muscle mass to underestimating it.
Estimates also become less accurate as the true GFR gets higher, so values of
60 mL/min/1.73m² and above carry more uncertainty (NICE, NG203).

## How protein in the urine should be measured

For detecting protein in the urine, NICE asks for an albumin-to-creatinine
ratio. Ordinary reagent strips should not be used in adults unless they can
specifically measure albumin at low concentrations and report it as an ACR,
and they should not be used at all in children and young people. A confirmed
ACR of 3 mg/mmol or more counts as clinically important proteinuria (NICE,
NG203).

## Reporting eGFR automatically

Whenever a serum creatinine measurement is requested, NICE asks clinical
laboratories to report an estimated GFR alongside the creatinine result rather
than the creatinine alone — so that kidney function is visible to the
clinician without anyone having to ask for it separately (NICE, NG203).

## Monitoring people on medicines that affect the kidneys

GFR should be monitored at least once a year in anyone taking medicines that
can harm kidney function, such as calcineurin inhibitors, lithium, or
long-term non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NICE, NG203). Never start or
stop any medicine on the strength of this — that is a decision for the doctor
who prescribed it.
